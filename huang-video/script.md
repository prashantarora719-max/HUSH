# Nvidia Without Jensen Huang: Paper Trillions, Entry No. 2 (script as recorded)

**Voice:** Jimmy  ·  **Length:** 7:32  ·  **Facts as of:** the afternoon of November 12, 2026, after Nvidia announced that Jensen Huang had resigned. Sections 2, 3 and 6 were recorded that morning, before the announcement.

## 0. Cold open

For thirty-three years, Nvidia had one boss.

As of today, it does not.

Jensen Huang has resigned. As chief executive. As president. And from his seat on the board. All three, effective immediately. The company says he left voluntarily.

He founded Nvidia. He was the face of the entire AI boom. And in less than a week, he went from untouchable to gone.

The finance chief, Colette Kress, is now running the company. But only as an interim boss. The board says it is still searching for a permanent one.

And that is the part that should matter to anyone with a retirement account. The most important company in AI has no long-term leader.

This is what happens to Nvidia without Jensen Huang.

## 1. A note on timing

A quick note before we start. This was recorded on November twelfth, hours after the announcement. The details will keep changing. The real question will not. Can Nvidia run without him?

## 2. How it got here

Here is what has been reported, and by whom.

On November eighth, Nvidia said its board had opened an independent investigation into its CEO. That followed reports that Huang had a relationship with an executive at a company that buys Nvidia's chips, and that American officials believe she was working with Chinese intelligence. China denies it. Huang told employees, in his words, "There is no excuse for what I did."

At first, the board backed him. It said it had confidence in Jensen, and no reason to believe company secrets were compromised.

Then, this week, the New York Times reported a second relationship, this time with someone who worked for him. And the Wall Street Journal reported that the board was preparing to remove him.

Those are reports, from people familiar with the matter. Nvidia has not confirmed them. And the spying claim in particular has not been proven. We are not going to dwell on the personal story.

The question for anyone who owns this stock is a different one.

## 3. Why one man matters this much

Most giant companies are built so that no single person matters too much. Nvidia was built the opposite way.

A typical CEO has maybe ten people reporting to them. Jensen Huang has more than fifty. No layers. No deputy. Every big decision goes through one desk.

And the decisions were his. Twenty years ago he bet the company on software that let its gaming chips do scientific math. Wall Street hated it. That bet is the reason Nvidia owns the AI chip market today.

So when investors paid five trillion dollars for this company, they were not only buying chips. They were buying his judgment.

Since the investigation was announced, more than four hundred and fifty billion dollars of that value has disappeared. And counting. *[CHART: the stock since November 8]*

That is the price the market is putting on one person.

## 4. Why he could not hold on

So how does a founder with that much power go that quickly?

Because he had less power than people think.

At Meta, Mark Zuckerberg holds special shares that give him control of the votes. The board cannot fire him. Nvidia has nothing like that. Huang owned about three and a half percent of the company. That made him rich. It did not make him safe.

Under Nvidia's own rules, a simple majority of the board can remove the CEO, with or without a reason. There were eleven directors. It would have taken six votes. The Wall Street Journal reported that the board was preparing to do exactly that. In the end, the company says, he resigned.

And there was a reason the board could not simply wait this out. Nvidia needs permission from Washington to sell its best chips abroad. A CEO under a cloud about China is a problem for exactly that. A senior member of Congress had already said he was prepared to subpoena him.

So this was never only about a private life. It was about whether the United States government could still trust the person running its most important chip company.

## 5. Who's next?

So who gets the job for good? The board says it will look inside the company and outside it. There are three kinds of answer.

The operator. Colette Kress. She has the job right now, as interim CEO, and she is still the finance chief as well. Steady, trusted by Wall Street. The open question is whether "interim" becomes permanent.

The engineer. Ian Buck joined Nvidia in 2004. He was a key architect of the software that made the AI boom possible, and today he runs the part of Nvidia that sells to the biggest AI customers. If you want someone who understands why Nvidia wins, it is him. But he has never run a whole company.

The outsider. Press reports have named people like Lisa Su, who runs rival AMD, and Rene Haas of Arm. Proven chief executives. But it would mean handing the keys to someone who was not there for any of it.

And then there is the answer Newsweek put in a headline this week. Who could replace Jensen Huang? Nobody.

## 6. What history says

That sounds bleak. So let us look at what actually happened to three other companies.

Intel, 2018. The CEO was pushed out over a relationship with an employee. The stock barely moved that day. But over the next few years, Intel lost its lead in making chips. The trouble was already underway. Losing the boss did not cause it, and did not fix it.

McDonald's, 2019. Same reason, same outcome for the CEO. The stock dropped about five percent. Then the new boss carried on, and the shares went on to new highs.

And Apple. In 2011, Steve Jobs stepped down, and plenty of people said the company was finished. His replacement was an operations man named Tim Cook. Apple is worth about ten times more today. *[CHART: three lines after the CEO left]*

The lesson is simple. It depends on whether the company's edge lives in one person's head, or in the system that person built.

## 7. Three things to watch

So here is what to look for.

One. How long the word "interim" lasts. An interim boss means the board does not have its answer yet. The longer that word stays, the more it tells you.

Two. Whether the engineers stay. Nvidia's lead is its people. If the top technical names start leaving, that tells you more than any press release.

Three. November seventeenth. Nvidia reports its earnings. They will be the first results the company has ever delivered without Jensen Huang in charge.

For thirty years, Jensen Huang told his employees the company was thirty days from going out of business. He was trying to prepare them for everything.

He never told them what to do if the thing they lost was him.
