## 0. Cold open

This company is worth one and a quarter trillion dollars.

On paper.

Last quarter, it brought in six point seven billion dollars. And lost twelve point three.

For every dollar it earns, it loses almost two.

It plans to spend eight hundred and fifty-six billion dollars on computing power. Roughly every dollar it expects to bring in.

And of every twenty people who use it, nineteen pay nothing.

This is OpenAI. And this is where the money goes.

## 1. The hole is getting deeper

One thing first. OpenAI is a private company. It does not publish its accounts. Every number in this video comes from figures it has shown investors, as reported by outlets like the Wall Street Journal and the Financial Times. *[DOC: the headlines]*

And those figures tell a strange story.

From the first quarter of this year to the second, OpenAI's revenue went from five point seven billion dollars to six point seven. Up eighteen percent. Good.

But its losses went from nine point three billion to twelve point three. Up by about a third. *[CHART: two bars growing, the red one faster]*

Most businesses lose less as they grow. This one loses more.

So where is it all going? Four places. And the fourth is the one to watch, because it is a number from OpenAI's own plan. We will get to it.

## 2. Where it goes, one: the free users

Start with the product you already know. ChatGPT.

About nine hundred million people use it every week. That is more than one in ten humans on earth.

Around fifty million of them pay.

So for every person with a subscription, there are about nineteen using it for free. And unlike a free video or a free search, every single answer costs real money to produce. A chip has to run. Electricity has to be bought.

To be clear, that does not make ChatGPT a bad product. Nine hundred million people do not come back every week to something useless.

But it does make it an expensive one. That is the first leak. The more popular it gets, the more it costs.

## 3. Where it goes, two: the promises

The second place is bigger. Much bigger.

To keep up with demand, OpenAI has been signing deals for computing power. Not for today. For years from now.

One deal alone, with Oracle, is reported at three hundred billion dollars. Sixty billion a year, starting in 2027. *[DOC: the Oracle headline]*

*[beat]*

Think about that. Sixty billion a year, to one supplier. OpenAI's revenue for the entire first half of this year was about twelve.

And computing power is not an idea. It is concrete and steel. In Abilene, Texas, there is a single site being built for OpenAI that is planned to draw one point two gigawatts of electricity. That is roughly the output of a nuclear reactor. For one campus.

Add it all up, and OpenAI's own forecast, reported by the Financial Times, puts the bill at eight hundred and fifty-six billion dollars by 2030. *[CHART: the number, then a bar beside projected revenue]*

Over those same years, it expects to bring in about eight hundred and forty.

Read that again. The computers alone cost more than everything it plans to earn.

## 4. Where it goes, three: the race

Why promise so much? Because of the third place the money goes. The race.

Every few months, someone releases a better model. To stay in front, you have to train a bigger one. And each one costs more than the last.

And OpenAI's lead is shrinking. A year ago, by one traffic measure, ChatGPT had about four out of every five visits to AI chatbots. This spring it was closer to one in two. *[CHART: 79% falling to 54%]* Google's Gemini took much of the rest.

It is still the biggest. But it is paying more every year to stay there.

## 5. So who is paying for all this?

*[re-hook, about the halfway mark]*

If you lose two dollars for every one you make, somebody has to cover the difference. So who is it?

Investors. In March, OpenAI closed a hundred and twenty-two billion dollar funding round, the largest in history. Eight months later, it is raising again, at that one and a quarter trillion dollar valuation.

But look at who the investors are.

Microsoft owns about a quarter of the company. It also rents OpenAI computers.

Amazon committed fifty billion dollars. OpenAI has agreed to spend thirty-eight billion on Amazon's cloud.

Nvidia put in thirty billion. OpenAI is one of the biggest buyers of Nvidia's chips.

*[CHART: three arrows out, three arrows back]*

And SoftBank, the Japanese investor, holds about thirteen percent.

Three of its biggest backers are also three of its biggest suppliers. If you watched our last video, you know this pattern. We called it the money loop.

So the losses are real. But as long as the checks keep coming, they do not matter.

And they have to keep coming. By one report, the money raised in March is expected to run out by 2028.

*[beat]*

Which raises the obvious question. Why would anyone keep writing them?

## 6. The case for OpenAI

Here is the honest answer. Because the other side of the ledger is extraordinary too.

At the end of last year, OpenAI was bringing in money at a rate of about twenty billion dollars a year. By this September, about fifty. *[CHART: 20 to 50]* Almost no company has ever grown that fast at that size.

In February, it started showing ads. Two hundred days later, OpenAI said that was already a billion-dollar-a-year business.

And remember those nineteen free users? Google and Meta built two of the most profitable companies on earth out of people who pay nothing.

This year, OpenAI expects about thirty-six billion dollars in sales. Its own forecast says that will reach three hundred and fifty billion a year by 2030. Ten times bigger, in four years. That is about what Google's parent company made in 2024.

So investors are not betting on what OpenAI earns today. They are betting it becomes the next Google, in four years. If they are right, one and a quarter trillion dollars will look cheap.

## 7. Where it goes, four: the number in the plan

Now, the number I promised you.

According to that same Financial Times report, OpenAI's own forecast shows it spending two hundred and seventy-eight billion dollars more than it brings in between now and 2030. *[DOC: the FT headline, the figure highlighted]*

*[slow]*

That is not the pessimistic case. That is the plan. That is what happens if everything goes right, including the part where it becomes as big as Google.

For comparison. Amazon, famous for losing money, lost about three billion dollars before it turned a profit. Uber, the biggest money-loser of its era, lost about thirty billion. *[CHART: three bars, the third one off the screen]*

OpenAI's plan is nine Ubers.

And in fairness, this is the improved version. An earlier forecast, from May, was reportedly even larger.

## 8. What has to go right

So here is the ledger, as honestly as I can write it.

For the paper trillion to become real, three things have to happen.

One. Investors have to keep funding it, year after year, through more than a quarter of a trillion dollars of losses.

Two. The cost of each answer has to fall faster than the number of answers grows.

Three. Those free users have to turn into money, through subscriptions or through ads.

Any one of those is possible. It needs all three, at the same time, for four years in a row.

And here is why it matters to you. OpenAI's finance chief says the company is aiming to sell shares to the public in 2027. When that happens, this stops being a problem for venture capitalists. It could land in your index fund.

One and a quarter trillion dollars.

On paper.

*[end]*
