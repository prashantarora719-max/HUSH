# Can Nvidia Fall?

**Working title options**
1. Nvidia Lost $1.7 Trillion in 5 Weeks. Is This the Fall?
2. Can Nvidia Actually Fall? The 5 Cracks
3. The Case Against the Most Valuable Company in History

**Format:** dramatic documentary, general audience, voiceover over motion graphics
**Length:** about 2,270 spoken words. Read straight through that is 15 minutes; with dramatic pauses and room for the visuals to land it runs 17 to 19. The timestamps below assume the slower pace.
**Facts as of:** 11 November 2026. Nvidia reports earnings on 17 November; see "Before you record" at the end.

Text in *[brackets]* is a delivery note, not to be read aloud.

---

## 0. Cold open (0:00 to 1:15)

On October sixth, 2026, one company was worth five point eight trillion dollars.

More than the entire economy of Japan.

Five weeks later, one point seven trillion of it was gone.

Not because the chips stopped selling. Last quarter, Nvidia made ninety-six billion dollars. More than double the year before.

The stock fell anyway. First, interest rates. Then, doubts about whether AI spending pays off. And then, an email from the man who built it all.

For thirty years, Jensen Huang has told his employees the same thing: "Our company is thirty days from going out of business."

It always sounded like a motto. This month, Wall Street is asking whether it is a forecast.

This is the case against the most valuable company in history. Five cracks. And what it would actually take for Nvidia to fall.

---

## 1. How it got this big (1:15 to 3:15)

To understand the fall, you need to understand the height.

In 1993, three engineers sat in a booth at a Denny's diner in San Jose and decided to build chips for video games. One of them was a thirty-year-old named Jensen Huang, who had once worked at a Denny's as a dishwasher.

Their first chip flopped. The company came within weeks of running out of money. That is where the thirty-days line comes from. It was not a slogan. It was the bank balance.

They survived, and for the next twenty years Nvidia was a successful, slightly boring company that made graphics cards for gamers.

But Huang made a strange bet. He spent billions building software that let scientists use those gaming chips for other kinds of maths. For years, almost nobody used it. Wall Street hated it.

Then, in November 2022, ChatGPT arrived. And it turned out that the maths behind artificial intelligence runs best on exactly the chips, and exactly the software, that Nvidia had spent fifteen years building.

*[pace picks up]*

What happened next has no precedent in business. In the year ChatGPT launched, Nvidia's revenue was twenty-seven billion dollars. Three years later it was two hundred and sixteen billion. Eight times bigger. And it kept seventy-five cents of every dollar as gross profit.

In April 2026 it became the first company to close above five trillion dollars. By October, it was within four percent of six.

One company was selling the shovels for the biggest gold rush in history. And that is exactly the problem. Because when you are the only shovel seller, everyone has a reason to want you gone.

---

## 2. Crack one: the customers are becoming competitors (3:15 to 6:00)

Here is an uncomfortable fact about Nvidia's business. A huge share of its sales comes from a handful of companies. Microsoft. Amazon. Google. Meta. Oracle.

Now imagine you are one of them. You are sending Nvidia tens of billions of dollars a year. And you know that out of every dollar you send, about seventy-five cents is profit margin.

What would you do?

You would try to build the chip yourself. And every one of them is.

Google has the TPU. Amazon has Trainium. Microsoft has Maia. These are custom chips, designed to do one job, running AI models, as cheaply as possible.

For years this was easy to dismiss. Custom chips were late, slow and hard to program. But Google's are now good enough that other companies want them. Anthropic, one of the biggest AI labs in the world, has reportedly committed more than a hundred billion dollars to run on Google's chips rather than Nvidia's.

*[beat]*

And there is a deeper shift underneath. Building an AI model, the training, is brutally hard, and Nvidia is still unmatched at it. But using a model, answering your question, is called inference, and it is simpler, repetitive work. As AI goes mainstream, inference becomes most of the workload. And inference is exactly where a cheap custom chip is good enough.

Now, the other side. Nvidia's defence is that software from the strange bet. It is called CUDA, and millions of developers have built their careers on it. Switching away is like asking a whole country to change its language. Many investors believe most custom chip projects will quietly fail, and that only Google's is a real threat.

They may be right. But Nvidia does not need to lose its customers to get hurt. It only needs them to have a choice. Because the moment a customer has a choice, that seventy-five percent margin becomes a negotiation.

---

## 3. Crack two: the money loop (6:00 to 9:00)

The second crack is about where the money comes from.

Follow one dollar. Nvidia invests in an AI company. That company uses the money to rent data centres. Those data centres are filled with Nvidia chips. The dollar comes back to Nvidia, and this time it is counted as revenue.

Critics call this circular financing. And it has grown fast.

Nvidia has put thirty billion dollars into OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT. Its own filings show it has guaranteed up to one hundred and five billion dollars of lease payments on data centres built for OpenAI. If OpenAI cannot pay, Nvidia is on the hook.

And look at one line on its balance sheet: stakes in private companies. By one analysis of its filings, that went from under four billion dollars to nearly forty-eight billion in a single year.

*[slower]*

Why does this matter? Because we have seen it before.

In the late nineteen-nineties, the companies building the internet's plumbing, Cisco, Lucent, Nortel, lent money to their own customers so those customers could buy their equipment. Sales looked spectacular. Then the customers ran out of money. The loans went bad, the orders vanished, and the sales turned out to have been borrowed from the future.

Is Nvidia doing the same thing? Not exactly, and the difference matters.

Most of Nvidia's sales still come from the richest companies on earth, paying cash. By one bank's estimate, the money loop touches around thirteen percent of revenue. The other eighty-seven percent is ordinary business.

But thirteen percent is not nothing. And it is concentrated in the part of the AI industry that loses the most money. OpenAI has made spending promises far larger than its income. If the customers at the centre of the loop stumble, Nvidia loses a buyer and an investment at the same time.

---

## 4. Crack three: the eight hundred billion dollar bill (9:00 to 11:45)

Which brings us to the biggest question of all. Who is paying for this, and how long can they keep paying?

In 2024, the five biggest cloud companies spent about two hundred and sixty billion dollars on buildings and equipment. This year, Morgan Stanley expects around eight hundred billion. Next year, more than a trillion.

A very large part of that ends up at Nvidia.

That spending is not a law of nature. It is a decision, made every quarter, by about five chief executives. And they will only keep making it if the returns show up.

So far the returns are slipping. By some estimates, the profit these companies earn on each new dollar invested has fallen from about forty percent to nearer twenty-five. And they are no longer paying for it all out of their own pockets. They are borrowing.

*[beat]*

Here is what makes this dangerous for Nvidia in particular. Nvidia does not sell a subscription. It sells equipment. If a customer who bought a hundred billion dollars of chips this year buys the same amount next year, Nvidia's growth is zero. If they buy twenty percent less, because the data centres they already built are enough for now, Nvidia's revenue falls. Even if AI itself is doing fine.

Economists have a name for this. It is a capital spending cycle. Railways had one. Fibre-optic cable had one. Every one of them ended the same way: the technology was real, it changed the world, and the companies selling the equipment still crashed, because the buyers paused to catch their breath.

Nobody knows when the pause comes. Demand today is still enormous. But Nvidia's share price assumes there will be no pause at all.

---

## 5. Crack four: China (11:45 to 13:45)

The fourth crack is one Nvidia did not cause and cannot fix.

A few years ago, Nvidia had about ninety-five percent of the market for AI chips in China. The second-largest economy on earth, and Nvidia owned it.

Then the United States government decided those chips were too powerful to sell to a rival. Round after round of export restrictions followed. Nvidia designed weaker chips to comply. Those were restricted too.

Jensen Huang has described the result in one sentence: "We went from ninety-five percent market share to zero."

Outside estimates are less absolute, and some sales have been allowed back under licence. But in its most recent quarter, Nvidia said sales of its main data centre chips to China were less than one percent of that business.

*[slower]*

Losing the sales is the small problem. The big problem is what filled the gap.

Chinese companies still needed chips. So they turned to Huawei. Analysts now estimate Huawei holds around half of China's AI chip market, built almost from nothing.

Huang has argued this for years: when you ban your best company from a market, you do not stop that market. You hand it to a competitor and give them a reason to get good.

Today Huawei's chips are behind Nvidia's. But it now has a protected home market, billions in guaranteed revenue, and the full support of its government. That is how a future global rival gets built.

## 6. Crack five: one man (13:45 to 15:30)

*[RE-CHECK EVERY LINE OF THIS SECTION ON RECORDING DAY. The story is days old and still developing.]*

The last crack is the newest, and the most personal.

Ask anyone who covers this company and they will tell you Nvidia is Jensen Huang. He has run it since the first day, more than thirty years. Dozens of senior executives report directly to him. The leather jacket, the keynote speeches, the long-term bets: investors are not only buying a chip company, they are buying one man's judgment.

That has always been the strength. This month it became the risk.

In early November, according to reports from several major news outlets, Huang sent an email to staff acknowledging that he had been unfaithful to his wife. The reports say the relationship was with an executive at a company that buys Nvidia's chips, and that American officials believe that person was working with Chinese intelligence.

Those are allegations. Nothing has been proven. What is confirmed is that Nvidia's board has opened an investigation, led by independent directors with outside lawyers, and that shareholders have filed a lawsuit.

*[beat]*

The stock fell more than four percent in a day, and more than five percent the next.

Set aside the personal story, because the lesson for investors is not about that. The lesson is that a company worth trillions of dollars moved by hundreds of billions because of a question about one person. That is what it means to be a one-man company. And unlike many famous founders, Huang has no special shares protecting his position. If the board decides he should go, he goes.

## 7. The ghost of Cisco (15:30 to 17:00)

So is this the fall?

There is one comparison every sceptic reaches for. Cisco.

In March 2000, Cisco was the most valuable company in the world, worth more than half a trillion dollars. It sold the essential equipment for the technology everyone knew was the future: the internet.

And everyone was right. The internet did change everything.

Cisco's shares fell more than eighty percent anyway. Twenty-five years later, they had still not returned to that peak. The company never went bankrupt. It is still large and profitable today. But anyone who bought at the top waited a generation and never got their money back.

That is the real meaning of a fall for a company like this. Not bankruptcy. A great business attached to a price that was too high.

But here is the difference, and it is a big one. At its peak, Cisco's shares cost around two hundred times its yearly profit. Nvidia's, after this drop, cost something closer to twenty-five. Cisco's price was built on hope. Nvidia's is built on profits that exist today.

So the question is not whether Nvidia's profits are real. They are. The question is whether they last.

---

## 8. What a real fall would take (17:00 to 18:45)

So let us be precise. There are three versions of falling.

The first is a correction. A sharp, painful drop, after which the business carries on. That is not a prediction. That is what has already happened.

The second is the Cisco outcome. The business stays strong, but the growth slows, the margins get negotiated down, and the shares go sideways for ten years. For that, you need two of our cracks to widen together. The cloud giants pause their spending, and their own chips become good enough to take the work that remains.

The third is a true fall, where Nvidia stops being the centre of the industry. For that you need almost everything: the spending pause, the custom chips, the money loop unwinding, a rival rising out of China, and a leadership crisis at the exact moment the company needs its boldest decisions.

*[slower]*

Any one of those, Nvidia can absorb. It has absorbed worse. It nearly died in the nineties. It lost half its value in 2008, and again in 2022.

What has changed is that, for the first time, all five cracks are visible at once.

On November seventeenth, Nvidia reports its next results. *[UPDATE this paragraph after the report.]* If the numbers are strong, this month may be remembered as the best buying opportunity of the decade. If they are not, it may be remembered as the top.

For thirty years, Jensen Huang has said his company is thirty days from going out of business. He said it to keep people scared when everything was going right.

Nobody at Nvidia needs reminding this month.

*[end]*
