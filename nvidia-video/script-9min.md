## 0. Cold open (0:00)

On October sixth, 2026, one company was worth five point eight trillion dollars.

More than the entire economy of Japan.

Five weeks later, one point seven trillion of it was gone.

Not because the chips stopped selling. Last quarter, Nvidia made ninety-six billion dollars. More than double the year before.

The stock fell anyway. First, interest rates. Then, doubts about whether AI spending pays off. And then, an email from the man who built it all.

For thirty years, Jensen Huang has told his employees the same thing: "Our company is thirty days from going out of business."

It always sounded like a motto. This month, Wall Street is asking whether it is a forecast.

This is the case against the most valuable company in history. Five cracks. And what it would actually take for Nvidia to fall.

## 1. Why this is your problem (1:10)

If you have a 401(k) or an index fund, you own Nvidia. It is one of the biggest pieces of the S&P 500. When it moves, your retirement moves with it.

And it got that big astonishingly fast. For twenty years Nvidia made graphics cards for gamers. Then ChatGPT arrived, and it turned out that artificial intelligence runs best on exactly the chips Nvidia makes.

In three years, its yearly sales went from twenty-seven billion dollars to two hundred and sixteen billion. It keeps about seventy-five cents of every dollar as gross profit.

It became the one company selling shovels in the biggest gold rush in history. And when you are the only shovel seller, everyone has a reason to want you gone.

## 2. Crack one: the customers (2:05)

Start with who buys the shovels. A huge share of Nvidia's sales comes from a handful of giants. Microsoft. Amazon. Google. Meta.

Now put yourself in their seat. You send Nvidia tens of billions of dollars a year, and you know most of it is profit margin. So you do the obvious thing. You build your own chip.

Every one of them is. And Google's are now good enough that other AI companies are choosing them over Nvidia.

Nvidia's defense is its software. Millions of developers are trained on it, and switching is painful. But Nvidia does not have to lose these customers to get hurt. They only need a choice. Because the moment a customer has a choice, that seventy-five percent margin becomes a negotiation.

## 3. Crack two: the money loop (3:00)

The second crack is about where the money comes from.

Follow one dollar. Nvidia invests in an AI company. That company uses the money to rent data centers. Those data centers are filled with Nvidia chips. The dollar comes home, and this time it counts as sales.

Critics call it circular financing. And it is not small. Nvidia has put thirty billion dollars into OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT. Its own filings show it has guaranteed up to a hundred and five billion dollars in lease payments on data centers built for OpenAI. If OpenAI cannot pay, Nvidia is on the hook.

*[slower]*

We have seen this before. In the late nineties, the companies building the internet lent money to their own customers to buy their equipment. Sales looked incredible. Then the customers ran out of cash, and the sales turned out to be borrowed from the future.

To be fair, this is not the same. Most of Nvidia's sales still come from the richest companies on earth, paying cash. By one bank's estimate, the loop touches about thirteen percent of revenue.

But that thirteen percent sits in the part of the AI industry that loses the most money. If it stumbles, Nvidia loses a customer and an investment on the same day.

## 4. Crack three: the bill (4:30)

Which leads to the biggest question of all. Who is paying for this, and for how long?

Two years ago, the biggest cloud companies spent about two hundred and sixty billion dollars on buildings and equipment. This year, Wall Street expects around eight hundred billion. Next year, more than a trillion.

A very large part of that lands at Nvidia.

And that spending is not a law of nature. It is a choice, made every three months, by about five CEOs. They will keep making it only if the profits show up. So far, by some estimates, the return on each new dollar is shrinking. And more of it is being paid for with debt.

*[beat]*

Here is why that is dangerous. Nvidia does not sell a subscription. It sells equipment. If a customer buys the same amount next year as this year, Nvidia's growth is zero. If they buy a little less, because what they already built is enough for now, Nvidia shrinks. Even if AI itself is doing fine.

Railroads went through this. So did fiber-optic cable. The technology was real. It changed the world. And the companies selling the equipment crashed anyway, because the buyers stopped to catch their breath.

Nvidia's stock price assumes nobody stops.

## 5. Crack four: China (6:00)

The fourth crack is one Nvidia did not cause and cannot fix.

A few years ago, Nvidia had about ninety-five percent of the AI chip market in China. Then Washington decided those chips were too powerful to sell to a rival, and blocked them.

Jensen Huang summed up the result in one sentence: "We went from ninety-five percent market share to zero."

Losing those sales hurts. What replaced them is worse. Chinese companies still needed chips, so they turned to Huawei, which analysts estimate now holds about half that market. A rival with a protected home market, guaranteed customers, and its government behind it. That is how a future competitor gets built.

## 6. Crack five: one man (6:50)

*[Re-check every line of this section on the day you record.]*

The last crack is the newest.

Nvidia is Jensen Huang. He has run it since day one, for more than thirty years. When investors buy this stock, they are buying one man's judgment.

In early November, according to reports from several major news outlets, Huang told employees he had been unfaithful to his wife. The reports say the relationship was with an executive at a company that buys Nvidia's chips, and that U.S. officials believe that person had ties to Chinese intelligence.

Those are allegations, and nothing has been proven. What is confirmed is that Nvidia's board has opened an independent investigation.

The stock dropped about ten percent in two days. Hundreds of billions of dollars, over a question about one person. That is what it means to be a one-man company.

## 7. The ghost of Cisco (7:50)

So is this the fall? History has one answer you need to hear, and it is not the one either side wants.

In March of 2000, the most valuable company in the world was Cisco. It sold the essential equipment for the technology everyone knew was the future: the internet.

And everyone was right about the internet.

Cisco's stock fell more than eighty percent anyway, and it stayed below that peak for more than two decades. The company never went bankrupt. It is still big and profitable. But people who bought at the top waited a generation.

That is what a fall looks like for a company like this. Not bankruptcy. A great business with a price that was too high.

But here is the difference. At its peak, Cisco cost around two hundred times its yearly profit. Nvidia, after this drop, costs closer to twenty-five. Cisco's price was built on hope. Nvidia's is built on profits that exist right now.

The question is whether they last.

## 8. What a real fall would take (9:00)

So here is the honest answer.

One crack, Nvidia survives. It has survived worse. It nearly died in the nineties, and its stock was cut in half in 2008 and again in 2022.

Two cracks at once, the spending pause and customers with a real alternative, and you get Cisco: a strong company and a lost decade for shareholders.

A true fall needs all five. What is new is that, for the first time, all five are visible at the same moment.

On November seventeenth, Nvidia reports its next results. That is the first real test.

For thirty years, Jensen Huang has said his company is thirty days from going out of business. He said it to keep people scared when everything was going right.

Nobody at Nvidia needs reminding this month.
