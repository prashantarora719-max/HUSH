# The Salad Oil Swindle: full script (draft 1)

Narrator only (Titan). About 1,860 spoken words: roughly 12 minutes at his sampler pace, with pauses and title cards.
Every figure is in `fact-sheet.md` under "Verified from sources". Lines marked † rest on one source or on sources that disagree; see the notes at the end.

## Cold open

For six years, men climbed these tanks in Bayonne, New Jersey, lowered a measuring line through the hatch, and wrote down what they saw.

Oil. Hundreds of millions of pounds of it.

American Express put its name on the paperwork. Fifty-one banks and companies lent a fortune against it.

In November 1963, somebody finally drained the tanks.

Forty feet of water. Two feet of oil, floating on top.

On paper, this one yard held as much vegetable oil as existed in the entire United States. Within a week, a Wall Street firm with twenty thousand customers was dead, American Express was fighting for its name, and the man behind it all, a former butcher from the Bronx, was nowhere to be found.

This is how he did it. And why, for six years, nobody looked down.

## 1. The butcher

His name was Anthony De Angelis. Everyone called him Tino.

He was born in the Bronx in 1915, the son of Italian immigrants, and he went to work young, in a meat and fish market. By 1938 he had his own hog-cutting business, and he was good at it.

He was also, from the start, a man who cut corners.

After the war he sold lard to Yugoslavia. The Yugoslav government sued, saying the product was substandard. He settled.

In 1949 he took control of a New Jersey meat packer called Adolph Gobel and won a contract to supply the federal school lunch programme. The government then accused the company of overstating weights and shipping two million pounds of uninspected meat. He settled that too.

By 1953 Gobel was bankrupt, and Tino was out as president. Investigators concluded he had been borrowing money against inventory that did not exist.

Remember that detail. Borrowing against goods that aren't there. In 1953 it cost him one company.

Two years later, he tried it again. This time, with someone respectable to vouch for him.

## 2. The perfect customer

On November 14, 1955, Tino opened the Allied Crude Vegetable Oil Refining Corporation, on a patch of the Bayonne waterfront full of old petroleum tanks.

His timing was perfect. American farms were producing far more soybean and cottonseed oil than the country could use. So Washington had created a programme called Food for Peace, which paid private companies to ship the surplus abroad.

Tino went after that business with a strategy nobody could explain. He paid farmers and crushers more than anyone else. He sold to the big exporters for less than anyone else. And he shipped it all by expensive rail to New Jersey.

Competitors could not work out how he made money. The exporters did not much care. They got cheap oil, and they earned interest lending him the cash to buy it.

By the late 1950s, Allied was supplying about three quarters of all the edible oil the United States shipped overseas. His loyal men drove Cadillacs.

There were warnings. In 1958 the Agriculture Department accused Allied of falsifying shipping papers on a contract with Spain. He paid one and a half million dollars to settle. Ten days later, the same department handed him a seventy-million-dollar order.

But the secret of Allied was simple. It was losing money on almost every pound.

To keep going, Tino needed cash. Rivers of it. And he knew exactly whose name could get it.

## 3. The name on the door

Here is how you borrow against a commodity.

You put your oil in a tank. An independent warehouse company takes charge of that tank, counts the oil, and gives you a receipt. You hand the receipt to a bank, and the bank hands you money. The bank never has to see the oil, because the warehouse company has vouched for it.

In 1957, the company vouching for Tino's oil became American Express.

American Express had a small subsidiary that did exactly this, called Field Warehousing. It was the one part of the company that lost money. Its manager was under pressure, and Allied was a big account.

So Field Warehousing set up shop inside Tino's own yard. And to run the warehouse, to guard the tanks and count the oil, it hired the people who were already there.

Tino's people.

Within a year Allied was doing seven times the business of the subsidiary's next-largest client, and the unit was finally profitable.

For the lenders, that was all they needed to know. A receipt with American Express on it was treated like a travellers cheque. Good anywhere.

They never visited the tanks. They didn't think they had to.

## 4. The trick

Oil floats on water. The whole swindle rests on that.

Some tanks were fitted with a narrow chamber directly under the hatch. Drop a line through the hatch, and it passed through oil all the way down. The rest of the tank held water.

Other tanks were joined by a maze of pipes. As the inspectors moved down the row, the same oil was pumped ahead of them and counted again.

And in many tanks, Tino didn't bother with any of it. He simply pumped in sea water and floated a little oil on top.

He rented more tanks through a second storage company. Of forty-one tanks on its books, twenty-three were leased to somebody else, seven were unfit to use, and one did not exist.

Somebody tried to stop it.

In June 1960, the manager of Field Warehousing began getting anonymous phone calls. The caller described the trick exactly: a chamber of oil, and a tank of water.

American Express sent a surprise inspection. It found unusual amounts of water in ten of seventy tanks. The explanation it accepted was broken steam pipes.

That November, the caller tried again, and reached the office of the president of American Express. Nothing was done.

By 1963, the receipts claimed nine hundred and thirty-seven million pounds of oil. About a hundred million pounds was real.

Then Tino stopped pretending altogether. He stole a pad of blank American Express receipts, filled in whatever number he needed, and forged the signature. In five weeks he wrote himself thirty-nine million dollars.

Why did he need that much, that fast?

Because he had made a bet. And it was going wrong.

## 5. The bet

In 1961 Tino landed a huge order from Spain and bought futures, contracts to take delivery of oil later, to cover it. Spain cancelled. He was left holding the contracts.

A cautious man would have sold. Tino bought more.

His idea was to corner the market: to own so many contracts that anyone who needed oil would have to pay his price. He spread his buying across brokers and dummy accounts to hide its size.

By August 1963 he held seventy-nine percent of the cottonseed oil contracts in New York, and a fifth of the soybean oil contracts in Chicago. The exchanges noticed. He was paying them more than a hundred thousand dollars a month in commissions. They let him carry on.

Almost none of it was paid for. Futures are bought on margin, a small deposit. If the price rises, you profit. If it falls, you must pay the difference in cash, by the next day.

Tino held about twenty-two thousand contracts, worth a hundred and sixty million dollars. A fall of a single cent a pound would cost him thirteen and a half million dollars. Overnight.

One of his brokers was Ira Haupt and Company, a respected stockbroker with little experience in commodities. Haupt had set strict limits on his account. In September, the partner who enforced them went into hospital. The limits were lifted.

All Tino needed now was for the price not to fall. By one cent.

## 6. Four days in November

Friday, November 15. Federal regulators open an investigation into his trading. The same day, the Senate shelves debate on a grain deal with the Soviet Union, and hopes of a big oil sale fade. Prices slip.

Monday the eighteenth. Rumours of bankruptcy reach the trading floor. Prices fall by more than a cent. Tino's losses for the day pass ten million dollars. At Haupt, a partner covers them with borrowed money, secured against the firm's own customers' shares.

Tuesday the nineteenth. Allied is bankrupt. One of the big exporters sends men to Bayonne to collect its oil. The tanks are empty.

Wednesday the twentieth. The New York Stock Exchange suspends Ira Haupt and a second firm. A lawyer for American Express tells the court that four hundred million pounds of oil is "missing". The FBI is called.

Thursday. The president of American Express tells reporters that if there has been some error, and he cannot conceive of one, insurance will cover it.

Friday, November 22. Haupt owes thirty-seven million dollars to its banks. Twenty thousand customers cannot touch their accounts. Wall Street is working on a rescue when the news comes from Dallas. The President has been shot. The exchange closes at seven minutes past two.

Over that weekend, with the country in mourning, the exchange does something it has never done. It puts up its own members' money to make Haupt's customers whole. The firm's partners lose everything.

In Bayonne, the tanks are drained. Water. Petroleum. Sludge. The shortage comes to one point eight billion pounds of oil, worth a hundred and seventy-five million dollars.

In Allied's office, the trustee finds six dollars in petty cash.

Fifty-one lenders were now holding paper for oil that never existed. And almost every sheet carried the same name.

## 7. The name on the door, again

Legally, the receipts were issued by a subsidiary. By the end of December it was bankrupt, facing two hundred and ten million dollars in claims. Its insurers refused to pay for fraud. American Express could have walked away. †

But American Express did not really sell travel. It sold trust. That year it issued five billion dollars in travellers cheques and money orders. If its name on a piece of paper could be worthless, why would anyone hold the others?

Its shares fell by a third.

The company's president, Howard Clark, told shareholders that American Express felt "morally bound" to see the losses made good. In time, it offered the creditors sixty million dollars.

Most of Wall Street saw a company in trouble. A thirty-three-year-old investor in Omaha saw one whose customers had not gone anywhere. Warren Buffett bought about five percent of American Express while others were selling. †

It became one of the most famous investments of his life.

## 8. The butcher's bill

When the tanks were opened, Tino was not there. He was found days later. Investigators traced half a million dollars to a Swiss bank account, which he handed back.

In January 1965 he pleaded guilty to four federal charges. He was out of prison by 1972. † Within a few years he was accused of another swindle, this time involving hogs. †

The reporter who unravelled the story, Norman Miller of the Wall Street Journal, won a Pulitzer Prize.

Tino never accepted the verdict. "No one in the world," he said, "ever did more for the economy of this country than I did."

Six years. Fifty-one lenders. One of the most trusted names in America.

All of them could have known. All it took was one man, one line, and the patience to let it sink past the first two feet.

## Notes on the daggered lines
- **"American Express could have walked away."** Sources differ on how far the parent was legally shielded; the manager testified that parent-company officers had a say in the subsidiary's business, which opened it to lawsuits. I will soften this to "its lawyers could argue the debts belonged to the subsidiary" unless you prefer the stronger line.
- **Buffett's stake.** "About five percent" is consistent across sources; the price is given as $13 million or $20 million, so the script gives no figure. He was born in 1930, so 33 in late 1963.
- **The sentence.** One source says 20 years, another 10; all agree he pleaded guilty to four counts in 1965 and was released in 1972. The script gives only the points that agree.
- **The hog fraud.** From Wikipedia only. The script says "accused". If I cannot find a second source, I will cut it.

## What changed from the sampler
The sampler's opening showed one inspector discovering the water with his measuring line. The sources say the tanks were found out after the collapse, when a creditor came for its oil and the tanks were drained. I have rewritten the cold open to match, which means re-recording those 50 seconds (about 900 credits). The paintings and the cut-away tank still fit.
