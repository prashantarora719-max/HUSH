[urgent, clipped, like a countdown] Friday, November fifteenth. Federal regulators open an investigation into his trading. The same day, the Senate shelves debate on a grain deal with the Soviet Union, and hopes of a big oil sale fade. Prices slip.

Monday the eighteenth. Rumours of bankruptcy reach the trading floor. Prices fall by more than a cent. Tino's losses for the day pass ten million dollars. At Haupt, a partner covers them with borrowed money, secured against the firm's own customers' shares.

Tuesday the nineteenth. Allied is bankrupt. One of the big exporters sends men to Bayonne to collect its oil. The tanks are empty.

Wednesday the twentieth. The New York Stock Exchange suspends Ira Haupt and a second firm. A lawyer for American Express tells the court that four hundred million pounds of oil is "missing". The FBI is called.

Thursday. The president of American Express tells reporters that if there has been some error, and he cannot conceive of one, insurance will cover it.

Friday, November twenty-second. Haupt owes thirty-seven million dollars to its banks. Twenty thousand customers cannot touch their accounts. Wall Street is working on a rescue when the news comes from Dallas. The President has been shot. The exchange closes at seven minutes past two.

Over that weekend, with the country in mourning, the exchange does something it has never done. It puts up its own members' money to make Haupt's customers whole. The firm's partners lose everything.

In Bayonne, the tanks are drained. Water. Petroleum. Sludge. The shortage comes to one point eight billion pounds of oil, worth a hundred and seventy-five million dollars.

In Allied's office, the trustee finds six dollars in petty cash.

Fifty-one lenders were now holding paper for oil that never existed. And almost every sheet carried the same name.
