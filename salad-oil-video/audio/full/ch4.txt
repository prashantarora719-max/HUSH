[conspiratorial, building] Oil floats on water. The whole swindle rests on that.

Some tanks were fitted with a narrow chamber directly under the hatch. Drop a line through the hatch, and it passed through oil all the way down. The rest of the tank held water.

Other tanks were joined by a maze of pipes. As the inspectors moved down the row, the same oil was pumped ahead of them and counted again.

And in many tanks, Tino didn't bother with any of it. He simply pumped in sea water and floated a little oil on top.

He rented more tanks through a second storage company. Of forty-one tanks on its books, twenty-three were leased to somebody else, seven were unfit to use, and one did not exist.

Somebody tried to stop it.

In June nineteen sixty, the manager of Field Warehousing began getting anonymous phone calls. The caller described the trick exactly: a chamber of oil, and a tank of water.

American Express sent a surprise inspection. It found unusual amounts of water in ten of seventy tanks. The explanation it accepted was broken steam pipes.

That November, the caller tried again, and reached the office of the president of American Express. Nothing was done.

By nineteen sixty-three, the receipts claimed nine hundred and thirty-seven million pounds of oil. About a hundred million pounds was real.

Then Tino stopped pretending altogether. He stole a pad of blank American Express receipts, filled in whatever number he needed, and forged the signature. In five weeks he wrote himself thirty-nine million dollars.

Why did he need that much, that fast?

Because he had made a bet. And it was going wrong.
