[tense, quickening] In nineteen sixty-one Tino landed a huge order from Spain and bought futures, contracts to take delivery of oil later, to cover it. Spain cancelled. He was left holding the contracts.

A cautious man would have sold. Tino bought more.

His idea was to corner the market: to own so many contracts that anyone who needed oil would have to pay his price. He spread his buying across brokers and dummy accounts to hide its size.

By August nineteen sixty-three he held seventy-nine percent of the cottonseed oil contracts in New York, and a fifth of the soybean oil contracts in Chicago. The exchanges noticed. He was paying them more than a hundred thousand dollars a month in commissions. They let him carry on.

Almost none of it was paid for. Futures are bought on margin, a small deposit. If the price rises, you profit. If it falls, you must pay the difference in cash, by the next day.

Tino held about twenty-two thousand contracts, worth a hundred and sixty million dollars. A fall of a single cent a pound would cost him thirteen and a half million dollars. Overnight.

One of his brokers was Ira Haupt and Company, a respected stockbroker with little experience in commodities. Haupt had set strict limits on his account. In September, the partner who enforced them went into hospital. The limits were lifted.

All Tino needed now was for the price not to fall. By one cent.
