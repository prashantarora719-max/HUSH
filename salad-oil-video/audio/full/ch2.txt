[engaged, a little wry] On November fourteenth, nineteen fifty-five, Tino opened the Allied Crude Vegetable Oil Refining Corporation, on a patch of the Bayonne waterfront full of old petroleum tanks.

His timing was perfect. American farms were producing far more soybean and cottonseed oil than the country could use. So Washington had created a programme called Food for Peace, which paid private companies to ship the surplus abroad.

Tino went after that business with a strategy nobody could explain. He paid farmers and crushers more than anyone else. He sold to the big exporters for less than anyone else. And he shipped it all by expensive rail to New Jersey.

Competitors could not work out how he made money. The exporters did not much care. They got cheap oil, and they earned interest lending him the cash to buy it.

By the late nineteen fifties, Allied was supplying about three quarters of all the edible oil the United States shipped overseas. His loyal men drove Cadillacs.

There were warnings. In nineteen fifty-eight the Agriculture Department accused Allied of falsifying shipping papers on a contract with Spain. He paid one and a half million dollars to settle. Ten days later, the same department handed him a seventy-million-dollar order.

But the secret of Allied was simple. It was losing money on almost every pound.

To keep going, Tino needed cash. Rivers of it. And he knew exactly whose name could get it.
