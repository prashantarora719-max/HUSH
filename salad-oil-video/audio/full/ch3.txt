[clear, explaining] Here is how you borrow against a commodity.

You put your oil in a tank. An independent warehouse company takes charge of that tank, counts the oil, and gives you a receipt. You hand the receipt to a bank, and the bank hands you money. The bank never has to see the oil, because the warehouse company has vouched for it.

In nineteen fifty-seven, the company vouching for Tino's oil became American Express.

American Express had a small subsidiary that did exactly this, called Field Warehousing. It was the one part of the company that lost money. Its manager was under pressure, and Allied was a big account.

So Field Warehousing set up shop inside Tino's own yard. And to run the warehouse, to guard the tanks and count the oil, it hired the people who were already there.

Tino's people.

Within a year Allied was doing seven times the business of the subsidiary's next-largest client, and the unit was finally profitable.

For the lenders, that was all they needed to know. A receipt with American Express on it was treated like a travellers cheque. Good anywhere.

They never visited the tanks. They didn't think they had to.
