# Why Fast Food Stopped Being Cheap: full script (draft 1)

About 890 spoken words, roughly 7 minutes with pauses. N = narrator, F = Frank, D = Danny, O = the owner, L = the Landlord.
Stage directions are in italics. Sources for every figure are in `fact-sheet.md` under "Second check".

## Cold open

*Counter. Menu prices tick upward as Frank walks in.*

**N:** This is Frank. Frank just ordered lunch.
**F:** Eleven dollars? For a burger, fries and a drink?
**D:** *(on his phone)* That's what they cost, Grandpa.
**F:** In my day this was a dollar.
**N:** It wasn't. But he has a point. McDonald's own numbers say its average menu price rose about forty percent between 2019 and 2024.
**F:** So where's it all going?
**D:** Beef. Wages. Everything's expensive.
*A man in a suit walks behind the counter. The manager hands him an envelope. He leaves.*
**F:** ...Who's that guy?
**N:** Good question, Frank. Somebody is taking your eleven dollars. We have four suspects. And the one with the best deal has never cooked a burger in his life.

*Suspect board: four silhouettes. Title: WHY FAST FOOD STOPPED BEING CHEAP.*

## Suspect 1: the food

*Frank holds the burger under a desk lamp.*

**F:** Talk.
**N:** Suspect one. The food itself. Beef prices have climbed for years, and the bun, the cheese, the cup and the wrapper all cost more than they did.
**D:** Told you.
**N:** In a typical restaurant like this one, food and packaging eat roughly thirty cents of every dollar. On Frank's tray, that's about three dollars fifty.
*Frank writes "$3.50" on a napkin.*
**F:** Three fifty. I paid eleven.
**N:** Exactly. The food is guilty. But only of three fifty.

## Suspect 2: the workers

*Frank squints at a teenager working the fryer.*

**N:** Suspect two. The people making it. Wages in fast food have risen fast. In California, the minimum for fast-food workers jumped to twenty dollars an hour in 2024.
**F:** Twenty bucks to salt fries?
**D:** You paid a guy forty to change a lightbulb.
**F:** That was a tall lightbulb.
**N:** Labour takes a little under thirty cents of every dollar. Call it three dollars ten.
*Napkin: "$3.50 + $3.10".*
**F:** That's six sixty. Where's my other four forty?
**N:** Now you're asking the right question.

## Suspect 3: the owner

*Frank kicks open the back office door. A tired man sits behind a tiny desk covered in bills.*

**F:** You. Mister McDonald.
**O:** I'm Gary.
**N:** Suspect three. The owner. Frank expected a yacht. But about ninety-five percent of McDonald's restaurants aren't run by McDonald's. They're run by people like Gary, who pay for the right to use the name.
**F:** So you're the one getting rich.
**O:** *(laughs, then stops)* Sit down.
**N:** Gary pays the staff. Gary buys the beef. Gary pays the electric bill, the insurance and the repairs when the ice cream machine breaks.
**O:** It's always broken.
**N:** After all of that, a typical owner keeps somewhere around five to ten cents of each dollar. On Frank's lunch, roughly fifty cents.
*Napkin: "Gary: 50¢". Frank looks at it. Then at Gary.*
**F:** Fifty cents. You're not the guy.
**O:** I'm not the guy.
**F:** Then who owns this place?
*Gary says nothing. He points at the ceiling.*

## The twist

**N:** Here's what Gary doesn't own. He doesn't own the recipe. He doesn't own the sign. And he doesn't own the building he's standing in.
*The walls turn into a blueprint stamped PROPERTY OF.*
**N:** In most cases the company finds the site, then owns or leases the land and the building, and rents it to the owner. And the rent isn't a fixed number.
**O:** It's a percentage. Of everything I sell.
**N:** Estimates put it around fourteen percent of sales, on top of a royalty of four to five percent. So before Gary pays himself a cent, roughly two dollars of Frank's eleven has already left the building.
*Two dollar bills float off the tray, up through the ceiling.*
**F:** *(watching them go)* ...Who's upstairs?

## Suspect 4: the Landlord

*The Landlord steps out of an elevator, catches the two bills without looking, and tucks them into his jacket.*

**N:** Suspect four. The Landlord.
**N:** Last year McDonald's collected about ten and a half billion dollars in rent from its restaurant owners. That's more than it made in royalties on the food, by a wide margin.
*A bar labelled RENT towers over a bar labelled ROYALTIES. The Landlord leans on the tall one.*
**N:** It owns the land under more than half of its restaurants and about four in five of the buildings. One estimate values the property at around a hundred and twenty billion dollars.
**D:** *(finally looks up from his phone)* Wait. So they're a landlord. That sells burgers on the side.
**N:** The line usually credited to the company's first finance chief, Harry Sonneborn, says it best: they are not really in the food business. They are in the real estate business.

## The reveal

**N:** And it shows. Most restaurants are happy to keep five or ten cents of each dollar. Last year the corporation kept about forty-six.
*A dollar splits into coins. Forty-six cents slide to the Landlord. Gary gets a nickel.*
**F:** Forty-six cents?
**N:** No fryers. No beef. No broken ice cream machine. Just the rent, the royalty and the name.
**F:** So that's why my lunch went up.
**N:** Careful, Frank. Not exactly.

## Why the price keeps rising

**N:** Gary sets the price on that menu board, not the Landlord. And Gary raised it because his beef, his wages and his bills went up.
**O:** I didn't have a choice.
**N:** But remember how the rent works. It's a percentage. So every time Gary raises a price to survive...
*The menu price ticks up. A coin drops into the Landlord's pocket each time. Ding. Ding. Ding.*
**N:** ...the Landlord gets a raise too. Without lifting a spatula.
**F:** He makes money when prices go up.
**N:** He makes money when sales go up. For five years, higher prices did that job.

## The crack

**N:** Until customers started doing what Frank is doing right now.
*Frank stands with his arms crossed, not eating.*
**N:** This spring, visits to McDonald's in the US fell about four and a half percent from a year earlier, by one tracking firm's count. The company says lower-income customers across the industry have cut back sharply. Over a third of its visitors come from neighbourhoods where the typical household earns under fifty thousand dollars.
**N:** So the company pushed an eight-dollar Big Mac meal and leaned on owners to hold prices down.
**O:** Guess who pays for the discount.
**N:** The owners have complained about exactly that. Because a percentage of a cheaper burger is still a percentage.

## Verdict

*Frank reads his napkin like a judge.*

**F:** Food, three fifty. Workers, three ten. Bills and adverts, a dollar seventy-five. Gary, fifty cents.
*He looks at the Landlord.*
**F:** And two dollars for the fella who owns the dirt.
**N:** Nobody here broke a rule. It's all in the company's own filings. But it explains why fast food stopped being cheap, and why it's unlikely to be cheap again. The price was never just about the burger. It has to feed the cook, the owner and the landlord.
**D:** So what do we do?
**F:** Next time, we're packing sandwiches.
*Frank finally takes a bite. Pause.*
**F:** ...It's pretty good, though.

## Tag

*The Landlord, at the door, tips an imaginary hat and speaks for the first time.*

**L:** See you next week, Frank.
*He drops a set of keys on the counter. On the tag: a coffee cup.*
**N:** Next case: the seven-dollar coffee.

## Notes for approval
- **Frank's $11 split is an illustration,** built from analyst models of a typical franchised restaurant, not a published McDonald's figure. On screen it will be labelled "illustration, typical restaurant".
- **Gary is fictional** and represents a typical franchise owner.
- **The Sonneborn line** is widely repeated but I found no original source for the exact wording, so the script says "usually credited to" and paraphrases.
- **"Seven-dollar coffee"** is a placeholder tease. It commits us to the next topic, so change or cut it if you prefer.
